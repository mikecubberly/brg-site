Event follow-up

How to turn B2B conference conversations into pipeline

To turn a conference conversation into pipeline, write down the buyer's reason to continue, the next step you both agreed to, and who owns it. Do that while the conversation is fresh. A booth visit, an accepted meeting, and a qualified opportunity each tell you something different. Record them separately so your team can pick up the right conversations and report what actually happened.

By Mike Cubberly, founder of Bottle Rocket Growth · November 13, 2026

Agree on what counts before the event

Pick a few outcomes that match why you're going. You might want to meet new buyers at target accounts, move open deals forward, or explore partnerships. Give each group a clear owner. Decide what would make a conversation worth a meeting and what evidence would let sales create an opportunity in your CRM. Use your existing opportunity criteria so the event gets measured the same way as the rest of your pipeline.

For example, a B2B software team might want to speak with security leaders who are consolidating vendors this year. A target account's security director and an implementation partner may both be worth meeting. They belong in different follow-up queues, and the partner conversation may have a longer timeline or a different owner.

Capture what the next person will need

When someone walks away from the booth or dinner, write enough that a teammate who missed the conversation could follow up without making the buyer repeat themselves. Set up a short form or a shared list before the event and make it easy to update from a phone. Ask for the next step directly and record the answer, including when someone asks to reconnect later.

Field What to record
Person and account Name, current role, company, and existing account or opportunity owner
Context Where you met and the topic they cared about, in a sentence or two
Qualification The project, problem, or timing the person shared and what remains open
Agreed next step The specific introduction, material, or meeting you both discussed
Owner and due date One person responsible and the date they should act
Current status Conversation logged, meeting scheduled, meeting held, opportunity created, or follow-up due

Here's an illustrative handoff. “Met at the partner reception. Their infrastructure team is reviewing cloud inventory before a renewal next quarter. We agreed to send a sample report Tuesday and offer two times for a 20-minute call with the infrastructure lead. Jordan owns the follow-up. Budget and evaluation process are unknown.” An account owner can act on that note without guessing what the buyer asked for.

Sort follow-up by what the buyer agreed to

Start with promises that have a date. Send the resource, make the introduction, or confirm the meeting with the relevant context. For an open deal, send the new information to the existing owner before anyone creates another thread. When the buyer expressed interest but made no commitment, send a short note that picks up the topic and offers an easy next step. Give partnerships their own queue and keep contacts with no next step out of the active meeting count.

A sample note for the fictional conversation above could read like this.

Good talking at the reception. You mentioned the infrastructure team is trying to get a cleaner inventory before the renewal. Here’s the sample report we discussed. If it looks close to what you need, I can walk through it with your infrastructure lead Tuesday afternoon or Thursday morning.

Count held meetings and opportunity movement

Review the event in stages. Start with qualified conversations, then look at meetings scheduled, meetings held, new qualified opportunities, and open deals that moved to a documented next stage. A meeting can have an event source even if it gets rescheduled twice. Use a single record for the meeting and update its status when it happens.

Keep event-sourced and event-influenced pipeline separate. A new opportunity that came out of an event conversation can be event-sourced under your attribution rules. If a deal already existed and the conference brought a new decision-maker into the evaluation, record how the event influenced it. A contact attending the show gives you a data point. An introduction that changes the deal gives you something specific to attribute.

Use the same event tag and a consistent reporting window so your next review can answer practical questions. Which conversations produced meetings that actually happened? Which of those advanced? What follow-up is still waiting on an owner? Start the review early enough to catch overdue promises and come back to it once the team has had time to qualify the opportunities.
