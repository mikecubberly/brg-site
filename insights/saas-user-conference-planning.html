How to plan a SaaS user conference

Start planning your SaaS user conference by deciding what customers should be able to do differently when they get home. That might mean getting a new workflow live, building a case for a rollout, or meeting peers who have solved the same problem. Give each audience a reason to attend, name the person who owns each part of the program, and decide how you’ll follow up before you open registration.

By Mike Cubberly, founder of Bottle Rocket Growth · November 13, 2026

Give each audience a reason to attend

List the people you want in the room and what would make the trip worthwhile for each of them. A hands-on admin may want time with a product specialist. Their executive sponsor may want to hear how another customer won support for a rollout. A prospect may want to see the product in a workflow that looks like theirs.

Audience What to put on the agenda Who owns the follow-up
Admins and practitioners Hands-on labs, configuration clinics, and peer sessions by experience level Customer success or enablement
Customer champions and executives Customer-led discussions on adoption, rollout decisions, and business outcomes The existing account owner
Prospective customers A clearly marked overview, a relevant use-case session, and optional product conversations The assigned sales owner
Partners Implementation or integration sessions with a specific way to collaborate The partnerships lead

Ask a few customers to review the draft agenda. Give them a realistic choice, such as a roadmap briefing or a live setup clinic at the same time. Their choices can tell you where an extra session or a second instructor would help.

Plan customer and prospect tracks around different decisions

Use shared sessions where customers and prospects both benefit, such as a customer-led walkthrough. Give practitioners room for detailed questions, and let prospects opt into sessions where the product and sales team are available. Make the audience and prerequisites clear in the session descriptions. If you promise a hands-on lab, spell out what attendees should bring and what they’ll leave with.

For example, a fictional workflow software company might run a customer lab where admins configure an approval rule in a sample environment. At the same time, prospective customers can attend an overview that walks through how a team chooses its approval process. The lab ends with a working configuration and a checklist. The overview ends with an option to book a discussion about the prospect’s own process. Use sample data in the lab so attendees can participate without bringing sensitive records.

Give every milestone an owner and a decision date

Work backward from the event. For a small one-day program, a starting schedule might look like this. Give each deadline a date and adjust it for the venue, budget, and speakers.

When Decision or deliverable Owner
12–16 weeks out Confirm audience, goals, budget, venue capacity, and program lead Event lead
8–12 weeks out Confirm speakers, publish the draft agenda, and open registration Program and registration leads
3–6 weeks out Check registration by audience, fill agenda gaps, and confirm meeting owners Event lead and account leads
Final week Rehearse demos, check access and logistics, and test the handoff workflow Event operations lead
First week after Deliver promised resources and introductions and review follow-up still due Each named account owner

Keep meeting requests and handoffs clear

Tell people at registration what communications to expect. Make it clear how they can request a sales conversation, customer success help, or an introduction to a partner. Before sharing someone’s contact details with a partner, confirm they agreed to the introduction. Use a private handoff with the right owner and record only what that person needs. That can be as simple as the topic, the next step the attendee agreed to, and the due date.

Measure the result each track was built for

Start with registration and attendance by audience. For customers, review outcomes like completed labs, agreed rollout steps, and scheduled working sessions. For prospects, track meetings requested, meetings held, and opportunities that meet your usual qualification criteria. For partners, record introductions completed and next steps agreed. Check promised follow-ups in the first week, then review progress after enough time has passed for people to act.
